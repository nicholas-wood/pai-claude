Calling that "prohibitive" is a strong word for a sub-1% one-time spend on an asset worth several hundred thousand.

The reframe that usually wins these arguments is separating two things the skeptics tend to blur:

The $125k buys shared EV-ready infrastructure (cabling, switchboard capacity, conduit, load management). It does not buy everyone a charger. Individual owners only pay for their own charger, if and when they want one. So the "I'll never own an EV, why should I pay" objection is answered: the shared spend protects the building's value and saleability for everyone, while the actual EV cost stays user-pays.

The cost-of-delay point is the other strong card. If the building doesn't do this collectively, you don't avoid the problem, you just hit it messily later. The classic failure mode is residents installing chargers ad hoc until the switchboard can't cope, then someone trips the building and you're forced into an emergency upgrade that costs more and gets approved under pressure. Doing it once, properly, with load management is cheaper per outlet than the piecemeal path, and the $125k won't get cheaper if you wait.

Now, the honest counter you should address rather than dismiss, because it'll make your case stronger:

The fairness concern is legitimate. Owners who'll genuinely never use it are subsidising the shared backbone, and for some owners (retirees, fixed incomes) a $2.5-3k lump sum can actually sting even if it's small relative to the asset. Don't wave that away. The answer is partly the value argument (the whole building's resale position improves, which benefits non-EV owners too) and partly structural: you can stage it, or fund the levy over time rather than as one hit.
